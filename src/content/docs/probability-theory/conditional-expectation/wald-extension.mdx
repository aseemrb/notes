[Wald's identity](../wald-identity) gives the *mean* of a random sum $Y = \sum_{i=1}^N X_i$. The natural follow-up: what is the *full distribution* of $Y$? Conditioning on $N$ and combining with [characteristic functions](../../independence/characteristic-function) gives a clean answer: the characteristic function of $Y$ is the **composition** of the generating function of $N$ with the characteristic function of $X_1$.

## Probability Generating Function

<Aside type="tip" icon="open-book" title="Definition: Probability Generating Function">
The **probability generating function** of a non-negative integer-valued random variable $Z$ is
$$
g_Z(t) \;:=\; \E[t^Z] \;=\; \sum_{n=0}^\infty t^n \, \Pr(Z = n), \qquad t \in [0, 1].
$$
</Aside>

The generating function uniquely determines the distribution of $Z$ (just as the characteristic function does for general distributions), and its derivatives at $t = 1$ recover the moments: $g_Z'(1) = \E[Z]$, etc.

## Theorem

<Aside icon="seti:smarty" title="Theorem: Wald's Extension">
<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Statement">
Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be i.i.d. with characteristic function $\varphi_{X_1}$, and let $N \ge 0$ be an integer-valued random variable with generating function $g_N$, independent of $\{X_i\}$. Set $Y = \sum_{i=1}^N X_i$. Then the characteristic function of $Y$ is
$$
\varphi_Y(t) \;=\; g_N\!\big( \varphi_{X_1}(t) \big).
$$
</TabItem>

<TabItem label="Proof">
Condition on $N$ via the tower property:
$$
\varphi_Y(t) \;=\; \E\!\big[ e^{itY} \big] \;=\; \E\!\Big[ \E\!\big( e^{itY} \,\big|\, N \big) \Big].
$$

**Evaluate the inner expectation.** On the event $\{N = k\}$,
$$
\E\!\big( e^{itY} \,\big|\, N = k \big) \;=\; \E\!\Big( \exp\!\Big( it \sum_{j=1}^k X_j \Big) \,\Big|\, N = k \Big) \;=\; \E\!\Big[ \prod_{j=1}^k e^{it X_j} \Big] \;=\; \varphi_{X_1}(t)^k.
$$
The second equality drops the conditioning by independence of $N$ and the $X_j$'s; the third uses [characteristic functions of independent sums multiply](../../independence/characteristic-function/#sums-of-independent-random-variables) on the i.i.d. sequence.

As a random variable in $\omega$,
$$
\E\!\big( e^{itY} \,\big|\, N \big) \;=\; \varphi_{X_1}(t)^N.
$$

**Take the outer expectation.** Recognize the right-hand side as the generating function of $N$ evaluated at $\varphi_{X_1}(t)$:
$$
\varphi_Y(t) \;=\; \E\!\big[ \varphi_{X_1}(t)^N \big] \;=\; g_N\!\big( \varphi_{X_1}(t) \big).
$$
</TabItem>
</Tabs>
</Aside>

The reading: **the characteristic function of $Y$ is the composition $g_N \circ \varphi_{X_1}$**. Random summation in time-domain becomes function composition in the transform domain.

## Worked Example: Geometric Number of Exponential Summands

Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be i.i.d. $\text{Exponential}(\lambda)$, and let $N \sim \text{Geometric}(p)$ on $\{1, 2, \ldots\}$ with $\Pr(N = k) = (1-p)^{k-1} p$, independent of the $X_i$. The standard formulas:
$$
\varphi_{X_1}(t) \;=\; \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - it}, \qquad g_N(t) \;=\; \frac{p t}{1 - (1 - p) t}.
$$

Apply the theorem:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi_Y(t) \;=\; g_N\!\big( \varphi_{X_1}(t) \big)
&= \frac{p \cdot \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - it}}{1 - (1 - p) \cdot \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - it}} \\
&= \frac{p \lambda}{(\lambda - it) - (1 - p) \lambda} \\
&= \frac{p \lambda}{p \lambda - it}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the characteristic function of $\text{Exponential}(p\lambda)$. So
$$
Y \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^N X_i \;\sim\; \text{Exponential}(p \lambda).
$$

A geometric number of i.i.d. exponentials is itself exponential, with rate scaled by the success probability $p$. The mean is consistent with Wald's identity:
$$
\E[Y] \;=\; \E[X_1] \cdot \E[N] \;=\; \frac{1}{\lambda} \cdot \frac{1}{p} \;=\; \frac{1}{p \lambda}.
$$

## Why This Is Stronger Than Wald's Identity

[Wald's identity](../wald-identity) gives only the first moment, $\E[Y] = \E[X_1] \, \E[N]$. Wald's extension gives the *entire characteristic function*, which by the [continuity theorem](../../convergence/continuity) determines the entire distribution. From it one can extract all moments (by differentiating), recognize known distributions (as above), and prove distributional convergence results for random sums.
