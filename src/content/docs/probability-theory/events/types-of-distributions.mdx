For a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ and measure space $(\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B})$, let $X$ be a random variable mapping a preimage $X^{-1}(A)$ to $A \in \mathcal{B}$. Recall that $\mu(A) = \mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(A))$.

## Equal in Distribution

If $X$ and $Y$ induce the same distribution on $(\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B})$, i.e., $F_X = F_Y$, then we say they have the **same distribution** or they are **equal in distribution**, denoted by:

$$
X \overset{ \text{d} }{=} Y
$$

<Aside type="danger" title="Important">
In general, $X(\omega) \neq Y(\omega)$ even when $X \overset{ \text{d} }{=} Y$.

**Example**: Consider $\Omega = \{H, T\}$ with $\mathbb{P}(H)=\mathbb{P}(T)=0.5$.
Let $X(H)=1, X(T)=0$ and $Y(H)=0, Y(T)=1$.
Then $X \overset{ \text{d} }{=} Y$ (both are Bernoulli(0.5)), but $X \neq Y$ everywhere.
</Aside>

## Absolutely Continuous Distributions

<Aside type="tip" icon="open-book" title="Definition: Probability Density Function">
If there exists a function $f$ such that for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$,

$$
\mathbb{P}(X \le x) = F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(y) \, dy
$$

then $f$ is called the <span class="text-accent">probability density function (PDF)</span>.
</Aside>

If a distribution has a density function, it is called **absolutely continuous**.
Note that this is different from just "continuous", which is defined below.

**Properties:**
For any $a < b$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}(X \in (a, b]) &= F(b) - F(a) \\
&= \int_{-\infty}^b f(y) \, dy - \int_{-\infty}^a f(y) \, dy \\
&= \int_a^b f(y) \, dy
\end{aligned}
$$

Also, $\mathbb{P}(X=x) \le \mathbb{P}(X \in (x-\epsilon, x+\epsilon]) = \int_{x-\epsilon}^{x+\epsilon} f(y) \, dy$.
Taking the limit as $\epsilon \to 0$, we get:
$$
\implies \text{for a r.v. with a density function, } \mathbb{P}(X=x) = 0
$$

This also implies $\mathbb{P}(a < X < b) = \mathbb{P}(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(y) \, dy$.

## Continuous Distributions

<Aside type="tip" icon="open-book" title="Definition: Continuous Distribution">
If a distribution has:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X=x) = F(x) - F(x-) = 0 \quad \text{for all } x
$$
i.e., its distribution function $F$ is continuous, then the distribution is called <span class="text-accent">continuous</span>.
</Aside>

**Relationship:**
*   Absolutely continuous $\implies$ continuous.
*   But, continuous $\;\not\!\!\!\implies$ absolutely continuous. (There exist continuous distributions which do not have a density function).

    **Example**: The **Cantor Distribution** is continuous ($F$ is continuous) but "singular" with respect to Lebesgue measure (derivative is 0 almost everywhere), so it has no PDF.

### Singular Distributions

A distribution is called <span class="text-accent">singular</span> if there exists a set $A \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $\lambda(A) = 0$ (Lebesgue measure is 0), but $\mathbb{P}(X \in A) = 1$, while $X$ is still continuous.

Look at the **Cantor set** or some fractals for examples.

### Decomposition

This is why a general distribution cannot be assumed to be a mixture of just a part with density and a part with probability mass on discrete points. There can also be "singular" parts.

A general distribution can be decomposed as:

$$
\text{General Distr} = \underbrace{\text{Abs. Continuous Part}}_{\text{with density } f} + \underbrace{\text{Discrete Part}}_{\text{with point masses}} + \underbrace{\text{Singular Part}}_{\text{continuous but no density}}
$$

## Examples

<Aside type="tip" icon="open-book" title="Examples">
1.  <span class="text-accent">Uniform distribution on $[0, 1]$</span>: $\text{Unif}[0, 1]$ or $U[0, 1]$.
    
    $$
    f(x) = 1 \quad \text{for } x \in [0, 1]
    $$
    
    $$
    F(x) = \begin{cases} 
    0 & x < 0 \\
    x & x \in [0, 1) \\
    1 & x \ge 1
    \end{cases}
    $$

    <img src="/assets/images/probability-theory/distributions/uniform-pdf.svg" alt="Uniform Distribution PDF" style="width: 100%; max-width: 400px; margin: 1rem auto; display: block;" />

2.  <span class="text-accent">Exponential distribution</span>:
    
    $$
    f(x) = \begin{cases} 
    \lambda e^{-\lambda x} & x \ge 0 \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
    $$

    Computation of CDF for $x \ge 0$:
    $$
    F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(y) \, dy = \int_0^x \lambda e^{-\lambda y} \, dy = -e^{-\lambda y} \Big|_0^x = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}
    $$
    (and $F(x)=0$ for $x<0$).

    <img src="/assets/images/probability-theory/distributions/exponential-pdf.svg" alt="Exponential Distribution PDF" style="width: 100%; max-width: 400px; margin: 1rem auto; display: block;" />

3.  <span class="text-accent">Standard Normal</span>: $x \in \mathbb{R}$
    
    $$
    f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}
    $$

    The distribution function $F(x)$ does not have a closed form expression in this case.

    <img src="/assets/images/probability-theory/distributions/normal-pdf.svg" alt="Standard Normal Distribution PDF" style="width: 100%; max-width: 400px; margin: 1rem auto; display: block;" />

4.  <span class="text-accent">Point mass at 0</span>. $\mathbb{P}(X=0) = 1$.
    
    $$
    F(x) = \begin{cases} 
    0 & x < 0 \\
    1 & x \ge 0
    \end{cases}
    $$
</Aside>
